# questions

I’d use AI less to generate more features and more to **expose assumptions, test trade-offs, and turn decisions into small experiments**. The context already has a strong north star: a pleasurable arcade truck, carefully authored stages, recurring people, and truck stops that make the road feel inhabited. I’d ask questions that protect that core.

## Questions I’d ask myself first

\1. **What should feel good even when nothing goes wrong?**

Can I enjoy steering, taking a curve, and watching the trailer follow on an empty road?

\2. **What is the one skill a player is learning?**

Is it primarily smooth driving, lane positioning, reading driver behavior, or managing the trailer? What should be secondary?

\3. **What makes a stage memorable besides its traffic?**

A curve, a landmark, a particular driver, a route choice, a spectacle, or the exit transition?

\4. **What does “authored and persistent” mean in practice?**

Which cars and events always appear in the same place, and which details can vary without undermining recognition?

\5. **What does the player need to understand at a glance?**

Can they recognize a safe gap, a merging car’s intention, the road’s shape, and the trailer’s position without reading UI?

\6. **What does the game promise after a mistake?**

Is a scrape a recoverable moment, a stop-and-talk event, or a serious failure? How can consequences matter without making experimentation miserable?

\7. **What makes returning to a truck stop worthwhile?**

What changes because the player helped, ignored, annoyed, or harmed someone—and what can change because they were kind?

\8. **What am I deliberately *not* building yet?**

For the first slice, can I defer crime systems, open-world simulation, and extra minigames until driving and returning to a stop are compelling?

\9. **What would make me personally want to play another run?**

A new stage pattern? A familiar driver? A better truck? A story consequence? A calm drive?

## Questions I’d ask an AI helping with design

Ask for **critique and evidence**, not just enthusiasm:

\- “What assumptions in this design are most likely to be wrong?”

\- “Which two mechanics are competing for the player’s attention?”

\- “What is the smallest prototype that could prove or disprove this idea?”

\- “Give me three versions of this feature: simplest, most distinctive, and riskiest.”

\- “What would a first-time player misunderstand here?”

\- “Find contradictions between these specifications. Don’t resolve them silently—list the decisions I need to make.”

\- “Which proposed numbers are guesses, and how could I tune them through a playtest?”

\- “What’s the failure case if this mechanic works exactly as intended?”

\- “Does this addition strengthen the core driving toy, the authored-stage structure, or the truck-stop relationships? If not, what is it for?”

\- “Turn this decision into a test: setup, observable behavior, pass/fail threshold, and what I should change if it fails.”

I’d also ask AI to keep a **decision log**: what’s decided, what’s still a hypothesis, and what evidence would change the decision.

## Questions I’d ask people who play the prototype

Avoid asking only “Did you like it?” Ask what they did and noticed:

\- “What were you trying to do when you moved the mouse?”

\- “When did you first understand how steering worked?”

\- “Which moment felt satisfying? What happened just before it?”

\- “Was there a moment you felt the game took control away from you?”

\- “Which car, if any, do you remember? What made it recognizable?”

\- “When did you anticipate something correctly?”

\- “What did you think the overpass ending meant?”

\- “Would you choose another drive? What would you hope was different?”

\- “What did you ignore because you were concentrating on something else?”

Watch their hands and decisions. Their behavior may be more informative than their explanation.

## The question I’d keep returning to

**Does this make the road feel more readable, more memorable, or more pleasurable to drive—or is it just more stuff?**

That question seems especially useful for your SMB3 inspiration: preserve a familiar grammar, then make each stage feel like a thoughtfully shaped place.
