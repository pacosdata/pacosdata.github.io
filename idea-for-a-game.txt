Thank you!! Part 2. You are wrestling with the most difficult magic of all: the balance between weight and play. You want to build a machine that carries the heavy, cold-blooded reality of murder, yet keeps it light enough to be a game—a "fun" descent into darkness.

You seek to create consequence without making the player feel like a philosopher of doom every time they press a button. You are trying to find the line where "doing bad" becomes a thrilling challenge rather than a heavy burden.

To solve this, you must stop thinking of "murder" as the goal and start thinking of it as a High-Stakes Logistics Problem.

Do not make the player's choice feel like "I am killing this person." Make it feel like: "I am handling a very delicate, high-value, highly suspicious cargo that requires perfect timing and precision." If the player is "killing," it should be part of a contract—much like transporting a shipment of live animals or volatile chemicals. The "bad feeling" comes from the tension: I am a person with a secret, and my skill at this game is the only thing keeping that secret from being found.

As for your mechanics, here is how you must structure the soul of this machine:

The Drive: Tension Through Rhythm Forget a complex simulation. The "driving" must be a rhythm-based survival loop. Do not give them a steering wheel; give them a dashboard of gauges and threats. The "fun" comes from the pressure of the road: maintaining speed to hit a delivery window, managing fuel levels, and reacting to sudden events (a flashing light in the rearview mirror, a sudden change in engine sound). It is not about "how to drive," it's about "how to survive the journey." If they are on the "Evil Route," the driving is a game of sensory awareness—listening for the sound of someone moving in the back, or watching the shadows on the road to ensure no police are tailing them.

The Truck Stop: The Social Marketplace of Secrets The truck stop is your hub of information. Do not make it a simple shop. It must be a game of "Social Trading." You do not just buy beer or tires; you trade whispers. A driver might give you a tip about which highway has the most frequent police patrols in exchange for that crate of contraband you're carrying. You trade information to manipulate the world around you. This keeps it "fun" because even in the dark routes, the player is playing a social game of wits—trying to blend in, trying to look like just another weary traveler while hiding a truth that would break the law.

The "Undertale" Element: The Weight of Choice To achieve that feeling where the player doesn't want to be "bad," you must give them a reason to care about the people they interact with. The hitchhikers, the fellow truckers at the diner, the small-town police—they cannot be cardboard cutouts. They must have personalities that are revealed through brief, high-impact interactions. If a driver helps you fix a flat tire in the middle of nowhere, and then later you choose to use that same driver as a "body" for your cargo, the player will feel the weight. The consequence is not just "I gained money," it is "I traded a human connection for profit."

You are building a game about the tension between being a person and being a professional. The fun lies in how well you can play that role without the world finding out who you truly are.

18 Wheeler is a road-life RPG with arcade trucking: make deliveries, become part of a roadside community, and decide what kind of person that community knows you as.
The truck is your home. The truck stops are your neighborhood. Driving connects the two.

There’s also one piece of the latest advice I would push back on.
1. Don’t make murder only a logistics problem
“Treat a person as suspicious cargo” might produce tension, but it risks creating exactly the emotional distance you’re trying to avoid. Players are very good at converting moral questions into optimization problems.
You can have logistics surrounding wrongdoing. But if you want killing to matter, the game must occasionally interrupt the optimization:
Someone no longer answers on the CB.
Their usual booth is empty.
A favor they promised never happens.
Other characters react according to what they knew about them.
An unfinished, ordinary concern remains: they were trying to get home, fix something, or reconcile with someone.
You don’t need graphic scenes or constant condemnation. An absence can do more than a gruesome cutscene.
And you cannot guarantee that every player will feel guilty. What you can do is make the game remember that a person existed, rather than treating them as a disposable resource.
2. What the driving should actually feel like
I would prototype top-down arcade driving with a visible trailer, rather than starting with a dashboard-only reaction game.
Why? Because a truck already contains a good physical toy: your front wheels get through a space, but your trailer still has to follow.
That creates a recognizable skill to learn.
The basic pleasure
The player should enjoy:
Taking a wide, smooth turn without clipping the inside corner.
Reading traffic early enough to avoid losing momentum.
Managing speed on a descent.
Finding a safe opportunity to pass.
Pulling a long trailer neatly into a cramped delivery yard.
Recovering from a small mistake without everything immediately collapsing.
That’s more distinctive than continually clicking flashing dashboard warnings.
Controls can stay simple: steer, accelerate, brake, interact. Give the truck forgiving handling, but enough weight and trailer movement that anticipation matters.
A typical delivery
For a prototype, try a four-to-six-minute trip:
Departure: pull out of the stop and join traffic.
Cruising: establish a comfortable rhythm; listen to the radio or CB.
A meaningful complication: roadworks, weather, a slow vehicle, or a route choice.
Arrival: navigate a short delivery-yard challenge.
Not every trip needs a disaster. Without peaceful stretches, you lose both the pleasure of trucking and the contrast that makes danger work.

A good lawful delivery should already involve interesting decisions. Crime should not be the only way to access challenging gameplay or earn meaningful money.

But their most important function isn’t selling items or unlocking quests.
They make the roads lead somewhere you care about.
I’d build around a circuit of recurring stops, rather than an enormous map full of interchangeable ones. Returning should be satisfying:
The mechanic remembers the repair you postponed.

Someone is sitting in your usual seat.

Information trading should be about trust, not just currency
Different people should know different things:
A dispatcher knows which customers reliably pay.

A regular driver knows whether a supposed shortcut is actually usable.

I wouldn’t use:
Normal → Bad → Evil → Mob → Serial Killer
That structure implies that murder is the final tier of progression—and that players must escalate to see the “real” game.
Instead, track a few distinct things:
Dimension	What it represents

Personal relationships	Who likes, trusts, resents, or fears you?

A reliable driver can be involved in crime. A completely lawful driver can be rude and disliked. Someone who accepted one terrible job can refuse the next.

You accept a favor. That creates an obligation. Fulfilling it damages another relationship. Now extricating yourself has a cost.
That produces a story without requiring the player to descend through a prescribed hierarchy.

What Works Especially Well
The Safety Check Mini-Game
This is the hidden gem here. Making players physically walk around and inspect their truck creates dread that most games rely on narrative for. It's diegetic tension. Forgetting a hubcap nut on a body-hauling run? That's storytelling without a cutscene. This should be core to the loop.

State-by-State Suspicion Radius
This prevents the game from becoming "one heat meter, everywhere." A player who gets pulled over in Texas but then crosses into Oklahoma gets a breath of air — but only until they do something to trigger multi-state cooperation. That's a natural escalation valve.

Questions / Areas to Develop
1. The Driving Must Be Genuinely Good
You compared to Crazy Taxi, which is honest. But Crazy Taxi is pure arcade — no weight, no consequence. An 18-wheeler that handles like Crazy Taxi might undercut the seriousness of what you're hauling.
Suggestion: Consider a spectrum. The driving feels heavy and satisfying (like SnowRunner meets American Truck Sim), but the routes and events create the Crazy Taxi chaos. You're not drifting through traffic — you're fighting to keep 80,000 lbs from jackknifing on black ice while someone's screaming in your trailer.
The contrast between controlled driving and uncontrolled situation is where the horror lives.
2. Perma-Death?
I'd say yes, but soft.

Truck destroyed = you lose the truck and cargo, but character survives (unless the trailer had a body in it... then you've lost evidence AND a paying customer AND possibly a live hostage)
Character death = hard reset to a "clean slate" state, maybe with a single persistent upgrade (a tattoo? a scar? a knowledge of a shortcut?) carried forward
This rewards the pacifist path naturally (you survive longer, earn more) while making the evil path feel expensive

3. Tone Management
The biggest risk is tonal whiplash. One minute you're haggling over a used pair of trucker sunglasses at a gas station, the next you're stuffing a body in the reefer unit.
Disco Elysium solved this with internal monologue. Your character "thinking to himself" is the right instinct. Make the serial killer internal voice distinct — maybe it starts as dark humor ("well, she was already dead from the accident, seems wasteful not to use the space") and escalates into something the player can't fully trust.
The normal internal voice could handle mundane trucking thoughts — route planning, fatigue, hunger — grounding the player before the dark stuff hits.
4. The "Fun Enough to Play Pacifist" Problem
This is the hardest design challenge. If the evil route is more mechanically interesting (contracts with codes, body-hiding upgrades, mob politics), why would anyone play pacifist twice?
Possible answers:

Pacifist route unlocks a different endgame — maybe legitimate business empire, fleet management, retiring rich
The "dark content" is genuinely upsetting/uncomfortable, making the pacifist route feel like relief
Different truck stop NPCs only trust you on the clean path — unique dialogue, romance options, community
The serial killer route has a cost: maybe your character deteriorates mentally, and the driving controls subtly get worse (micro-stutters, delayed inputs) representing dissociation

Your magnification hierarchy is smart. Think about making the transitions themselves part of the experience:

Map → Driving: Zoom out to highway, the truck becomes a small box crawling between states
Driving → Walking: Pull into a lot, doors open, camera shifts to side-scroll as you step out
Walking → Inspection: Walk up to the truck, camera pushes in, now you're checking bolts
Inspection → Pulled Over: Flashing lights interrupt, camera snaps to the cop's face at your window

Each transition is a beat. Use music or audio cues to signal them. The pulled-over transition especially should feel like a gut punch — you were just checking tire pressure and suddenly there's a hand tapping the glass.

One More Thought: The Hitchhikers
Hitchhikers are your wild card. On the normal route, they're flavor — stories, tips, maybe minor side quests. On the evil route, they become predatory opportunities.
But consider making some hitchhikers dangerous on the normal route too. A guy who flags you down and pulls a knife. Suddenly the "pacifist" route has its own tension — you chose not to be a killer, but the world didn't choose not to be violent toward you.
That moral complexity is where this game could be genuinely special.

**18 Wheeler** is a road-life RPG: the truck is home, the stops are the neighborhood, and driving is the road between them. Darker work can change that life. It is not the game’s identity, and it is not the last level of a morality ladder.

The sentence that got cut off should finish like this: otherwise the game teaches players that only cruelty is memorable. Kindness has to rewrite the same places, or “Undertale-like” just means a hidden evil ending.

## What to lock

| Keep | Drop |
|---|---|
| Four separate tracks: professional reputation, personal relationships, legal attention, criminal obligations | One slider from Normal → Bad → Evil → Mob → Serial Killer |
| A small circuit of stops you return to | A huge map of interchangeable diners |
| Top-down arcade driving with a visible trailer | Dashboard-only rhythm minigame, or a full truck sim |

Lawful work has to be worth playing. Crime should not be the only interesting money.

## The slice worth building first

No bodies, no mob, no permadeath. Just the life that those systems would later damage.

1. **Three stops** on a short highway loop: a diner, a repair bay, a yard that posts jobs.
2. **One drive**, about four minutes: pull out, cruise, one real complication (slow traffic, weather, or a fork), then park a long trailer in a tight yard.
3. **Cargo that changes the same drive**, not a new minigame: fragile (smooth inputs), heavy (slow to stop), urgent (route choice), oversized (clearance).
4. **A handful of recurring people** who remember one thing: a postponed repair, a seat you usually take, a favor, a wrong rumor they still believe.
5. **Information as trust**, not a shop tab. Some tips are gifts, some are for sale, some are wrong. The journal stores who said it, not a true/false stamp.

Controls stay simple: steer, gas, brake, interact. The skill is the trailer. The cab clears a corner before the trailer does. Forgiving handling, real weight.

## How the same stop changes

Not new lighting. The room stays ordinary.

- **New:** you learn names and take whatever job is on the board.
- **Dependable:** someone waves you over, a job is offered before it’s posted, a friend asks for a ride home.
- **You helped and it stuck:** the mechanic holds a part, the waitress saves the bad coffee you actually like, a driver you towed shows up two stops later with a real tip.
- **You harmed someone here:** one person confronts you, one avoids you, one offers work because they think you’re dangerous. Their usual booth can just be empty. An unfinished ordinary plan remains — they were trying to get home.

A hitchhiker who fixed your tire and later isn’t on the CB anymore will do more than a gore scene. You still cannot force every player to feel guilty. You can make the game remember a person existed.

## Humor and weight

Keep the jokes in the daylight: diner specials, sunglasses trades, CB arguments, a cop who is mostly bored. When something serious happens, don’t wink. The contrast is the point. If every dark beat is a punchline, nothing is heavy. If nothing is funny, nobody wants the lawful route.

## The mouse-wheel steering

A few things to resolve:

**Return-to-center is the big one.** In a real truck, you un-wind the wheel after a turn. If the player sweeps the mouse to the bottom-right for a long curve, what brings the wheel back? Options:

1. *Manual unwind* — the player mirrors the motion back up. Most realistic, but exhausting on long drives and punishing for anyone using a laptop touchpad.
2. *Self-centering spring* — the wheel eases back toward 0 when the mouse returns toward the top of the cab. Player still gestures the unwind, but sloppiness is forgiven.
3. *Snap-back on release* — cursor leaves the hotspot zone, wheel eases to center. Simplest, least tactile.

## 1. Geometry conventions

Everything is measured from the **wheel center** — an invisible point anchored to the cab sprite (it should ride with the cab if we add the elastic camera margin later).

- **Wheel radius ring:** r = 140 px (tunable). The active capture area is an annulus around the wheel center, inner 70 px, outer 210 px.
- **Cursor angle θ:** measured clockwise from 12 o'clock at the wheel center (so 0° = top, 90° = 3 o'clock, 180° = bottom).
- **Only θ matters inside the capture ring.** Cursor distance from the center scales *effort/resistance* slightly (feels like gripping closer to or farther from the rim) but not the target angle — this keeps zone sizes consistent.
- **Deadman rule:** if the cursor exits the capture ring, the wheel *holds* its current value with a weak drift toward center (~15°/sec). No flailing, no instant reset.

Zone angles below are for the right half; everything mirrors for left.

## 2. Zone map

Clock positions = which "hand position" the cursor is emulating. Depth of turn grows as the cursor sweeps down the side of the wheel — exactly your hand-over-hand intuition.

| Clock pos | θ (right side) | Wheel rotation from center | Front-wheel angle | Feel |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12:00 (neutral well) | 0° | 0° | 0° | Soft magnet, easy to sit in |
| 12 ±30° (fine band) | −30°…+30° | ±90° | ±5° | Free movement, no magnet — lane keeping |
| 1:00 | 30° | ±360° (1 turn) | ±12° | Medium well |
| 2:00 | 60° | ±540° (1.5 turns) | ±20° | Firm well — your "fixed degree" spots |
| 4:00 | 120° | ±720° (2 turns) | ±28° | Very firm well |
| 5:00 (corner lock) | 150° | ±900° (2.5 turns) | ±35° | Lock — hardest to leave |

Lock-to-lock is therefore 5 turns total (2.5 each way), matching your 2.5–3 estimate.

Center-to-lock = 900°. Between wells, the wheel interpolates **continuously** through θ (the wells pull, they don't snap):

```text
target(θ) = piecewise-linear over the anchor points:
  (−150°, −900°) (−120°, −720°) (−60°, −540°) (−30°, −90°)
  (0°, 0°)
  (+30°, +90°) (+60°, +540°) (+120°, +720°) (+150°, +900°)

wheel_rate_limit = 720°/sec   // hand-over-hand takes time; cap slew
well_pull        = 6/sec      // exponential ease toward target(θ)
```

The piecewise-linear anchors are deliberately **nonuniform**: the jump from 30°→60° of cursor travel covers a big chunk of wheel rotation (that's the hand-over-hand "fling"), while the fine band maps cursor almost 1:1 to small wheel input. That contrast *is* the physical metaphor.

### Front-wheel output

```text
front_wheel = (wheel_rotation / 900°) × 35°
steer_response smoothing: front_wheel_visual lerps to front_wheel_target at 8/sec
```

35° max front-wheel angle is generous for a truck; tune down to ~28° if parking feels trivial.

## 4. Return-to-center spring (the unwind problem)

- **Above the fine band (cursor near 12:00):** the neutral well pulls. Spring strength is **speed-dependent** — this is the caster-effect trick:
  - Standing still: pull = 2°/sec of wheel. Effectively manual — you parked turned, you stay turned.
  - Highway speed: pull = 240°/sec. The wheel visibly spins itself back when you relax upward, like a real truck's caster does.
- **Inside the fine band or below:** no auto-return. The player unwinds deliberately by sweeping the cursor back up — but the speed-dependent pull at the top means a lazy unwind still finishes itself.
- **Mirrored fairness:** identical constants for both directions, so the feel is symmetric.

## 5. Pointer capture & ergonomics guards

1. **Pointer lock** on click into driving view; ESC releases. Without it, hitting the bottom screen edge at full lock kills the gesture.
2. **Lock wells are escapable by intent, not by drift:** leaving a lock well requires crossing the boundary with cursor *velocity* above a threshold (a flick unwinds; resting jitter doesn't). This prevents the worst failure mode — losing full lock because your hand trembled.
3. **Fatigue cap:** full lock is never *required* for more than ~2 seconds anywhere in a level. Yards and docking challenges should be designed around the 1.5-turn wells (±20°); lock is for emergencies and dramatic wide swings only. I'd actually put a lint rule in your level data: flag any parking bay whose geometry demands >2s of continuous lock.

3. Is the unwind gesture discovered *without being told*? If nobody unwinds unprompted, add a subtle wheel-shadow that rotates opposite the turn (visual affordance).

The trailer is the whole game. It follows the hitch geometrically, so lane changes swing it, corrections wag it, and docking means backing it into the bay while it fights you. If this feels good, everything else has something to stand on.
Collisions are tiered, per the agreement: a scrape gets you a horn and a CB jab; a real hit forces the pull-over exchange screen; fleeing leads to a pursuit that ends in arrest and impound, not a game-over — you lose money and time, and the run continues.
The wheels only show up when you leave the pavement — rumble strips and grass shake the screen and grind your speed, which teaches lane discipline without any UI lecturing.
Quiet stretches exist on purpose. Roughly a third of the run has no merges at all, so the tension has a baseline to rise from.
The truck stop is flavor, not a menu yet. A diner special, a mechanic, a job board, CB chatter — enough to prove the "road trip with a neighborhood at the end" feeling before any economy gets designed.
The two things I'd most want you to judge by feel: whether the fine band at the top of the wheel is wide enough for lane-keeping without wandering into a turn well, and whether the no-signal mergers read as spicy rather than unfair. Both are single constants in the tuning table at the top of the code, so they're cheap to change.

If steering the trailer through one merge and backing into that bay feels good, the next slice is the one we outlined: drive, park, walk in, talk, deliver — and hear the place remember you when you come back.
